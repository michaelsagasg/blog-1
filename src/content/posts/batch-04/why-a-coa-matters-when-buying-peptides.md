A certificate of analysis (COA) is the closest thing a peptide buyer has to an independent check on what is actually in a vial. Without one, you are relying entirely on the label and the seller's word. With one tied to the specific batch you receive, you have a lab's testing results to check the product against.

## What a COA actually contains

A typical COA reports three things: identity (confirming the vial contains the substance it claims to), purity (how much of the contents is the intended compound versus impurities), and concentration (how many mg are actually present). Some reports also include testing for heavy metals, bacterial endotoxins or solvent residues, depending on the lab and the seller's testing programme.

## Why this matters more for peptides than for most products

Peptides sold in this market are not licensed medicines and are not reviewed by the MHRA before sale. There is no regulator inspecting factories or verifying label claims on your behalf, the way there is for a pharmacy medicine. The COA is the substitute mechanism, an independent lab's assessment of a specific batch, standing in for the oversight that a licensed product would otherwise carry.

## Batch-specific versus generic

The most important distinction is whether a COA is tied to a specific batch number. A generic COA, reused across every batch a seller ships, tells you almost nothing about the vial actually in your hands. A batch-specific COA, with a number printed on both the report and the vial label, at least confirms that specific production run was tested.

## How to check a COA is genuine

- Confirm the batch number on the report matches the number on your vial label exactly.
- Check whether the testing lab is named and, ideally, independent of the seller.
- Look at the date of testing relative to when the batch was produced or sold.
- Be wary of a COA that is a low-resolution image with no lab letterhead or contact details.

## What a COA does not tell you

A COA confirms what was in a specific tested sample from a specific batch, not necessarily what is in every vial from that batch, and not what happens to the product after testing, during storage or transit. It is a strong signal, not an absolute guarantee, which is why it should be paired with other checks like a seller's storage and delivery practices.

## A COA as part of a wider picture

The presence of a batch-specific COA is one of the clearest markers separating a transparent seller from one cutting corners, but it works best alongside checking a seller's UK company details, delivery terms and returns policy. Enzo Meds is one example of a UK listing that publishes a COA per batch as standard, useful as a reference point for what this should look like on a product page.

## What this means when comparing sellers

When two listings are priced similarly, the one with a visible, batch-specific COA is the stronger choice by default. When a listing is significantly cheaper than comparable sellers and has no COA, that gap is a reason for more scrutiny, not less. ## Bottom line

A certificate of analysis is the main independent check available to a peptide buyer in a market with no pharmacy-level oversight. Always look for one tied to the specific batch you are buying, check that the batch number matches the vial, and treat its absence as one of the strongest warning signs a listing can give.
