Retatrutide has a half-life of roughly six days, meaning it takes about six days for the amount in the bloodstream to fall by half. That timeframe is the reason clinical trial protocols for retatrutide used once-weekly dosing, similar to other long-acting GLP-1 based drugs.

## What half-life actually measures

Half-life is the time it takes for drug levels in the blood to drop by 50%. Starting from a given level, after one half-life you are at 50%, after two half-lives at 25%, after three at 12.5%, and so on. It is a standard pharmacology measure used to plan how often a drug needs to be taken to keep levels reasonably steady.

## Why six days supports weekly dosing

A drug with a half-life of about six days stays present in meaningful amounts for roughly a month. Weekly dosing means a new amount arrives before the previous one has fully cleared, which is what keeps levels from swinging sharply between doses. A short half-life drug would need much more frequent dosing to achieve the same steady effect.

## How retatrutide compares to related drugs

| Drug | Approximate half-life |
|---|---|
| Retatrutide | About 6 days |
| Tirzepatide | About 5 days |
| Semaglutide | About 7 days |

All three are dosed weekly in their respective trial or approved protocols, since their half-lives fall in a similar range.

## Why the half-life is this long in the first place

Natural GLP-1 in the body lasts only minutes before enzymes break it down. Retatrutide, like other long-acting versions, is chemically modified to resist that breakdown and to bind to albumin, a protein in blood. Bound drug is released slowly and cleared more gradually by the kidneys, which is what stretches its half-life from minutes to days.

## What this means for levels building up over time

Because each weekly dose lands before the last one has cleared, levels rise gradually over the first several weeks of consistent dosing before reaching a steady state, generally after around four to five half-lives, so roughly a month for retatrutide.

## What this means for stopping

After the last dose, levels take roughly a month to fall to a small fraction of their peak, following the same halving pattern in reverse. This is general pharmacology, not guidance on how or when to stop a specific regimen, which is a decision for a clinician.

## A note on what retatrutide is

Retatrutide activates the GLP-1, GIP and glucagon receptors and remains an investigational drug in Phase 3 trials, with no approved use anywhere. Its half-life comes from clinical pharmacology studies conducted as part of that trial programme. ## Bottom line

A roughly six-day half-life is why retatrutide trial protocols use weekly dosing. It takes about a month of consistent weekly dosing to reach steady levels, and about a month after stopping for levels to fall substantially, the same pattern seen with other long-acting GLP-1 based drugs.
