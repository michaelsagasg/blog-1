A noticeable slowdown in the rate of weight loss around the third month is a pattern commonly reported anecdotally by people using GLP-1 based drugs, including retatrutide, though it is not a distinct phase specifically defined in Eli Lilly's published trial data. The trial data shows a generally continuing, though gradually slowing, rate of weight loss over time rather than a sharp plateau at a fixed point.

## What the trial data actually shows over time

In Eli Lilly's Phase 2 trial, weight loss on retatrutide continued through the full 48-week study period at the higher doses, without the curve flattening completely. The rate of loss was generally faster in the earlier months and more gradual later, a pattern common across weight-related interventions rather than something unique to a "month 3" milestone specifically.

## Why an early slowdown is physiologically plausible

As body weight decreases, resting energy expenditure, the amount of energy the body burns at rest, tends to decrease too, since a smaller body generally requires less energy to maintain. This means the same calorie intake that supported faster loss earlier may support a slower rate of loss later, independent of anything about the drug itself. This is a well-documented pattern in weight loss research generally, not specific to retatrutide.

## Dose escalation timing and perceived plateaus

Retatrutide trial protocols used a staged dose increase, typically every four weeks. Some community reports describe a perceived plateau coinciding with the period between one dose increase and the next, as the body adjusts to a given dose before the next increase takes effect. This is a reported pattern, not something formally studied as a distinct "plateau" phenomenon.

## What community reports describe

Discussions among people using GLP-1 and multi-agonist drugs, including retatrutide, often mention a slower period around two to three months in, sometimes followed by continued loss afterward. These reports are self-reported and not clinical data, and individual experiences vary considerably.

## What a plateau does not necessarily mean

A slower rate of change does not necessarily mean the drug has stopped having an effect. Appetite suppression and other mechanisms can continue while the rate of weight change simply decelerates, consistent with how weight loss tends to behave more broadly as a person's body composition changes over time.

## What is not known

There is no retatrutide-specific research that isolates why some people report a plateau around month three specifically, whether it relates to dose timing, metabolic adaptation, or other individual factors, or how consistently this pattern occurs across the wider trial population versus anecdotal reports.

## What this means going forward

Because retatrutide remains an investigational drug studied primarily through 48-week Phase 2 data, longer-term patterns beyond a year are not yet well established in published research. Ongoing Phase 3 trials are expected to add more data on this over time.

## Bottom line

A slowdown in weight loss around the third month is commonly reported anecdotally but is not defined as a distinct phase in retatrutide's published trial data, which shows a generally continuing though gradually decelerating rate of loss. Decreasing resting energy expenditure as weight drops is a well-established, general explanation for why loss often slows over time, independent of the specific drug used.
