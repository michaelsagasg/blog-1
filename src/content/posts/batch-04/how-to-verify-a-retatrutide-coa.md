Verifying a retatrutide certificate of analysis (COA) means checking four things: the batch number matches your vial, the testing lab is named, the test date makes sense against when the batch was produced, and the purity and concentration figures are actually reported, not just summarised as "pass." Work through these before you rely on any COA a seller shows you.

## Step 1: Match the batch number

The batch or lot number on the COA should match the number printed on your vial's label exactly. If a seller shows a COA with no batch number at all, or one that does not match what you receive, the report is not verifying the product in front of you, whatever else it says.

## Step 2: Check who did the testing

A credible COA names the testing laboratory, ideally one independent of the seller, along with some way to confirm that lab exists, such as a website or accreditation number. A report with no named lab, or one that only says "tested in-house" with no further detail, carries far less weight.

## Step 3: Look at the test date

Compare the testing date on the COA against how long the seller has been shipping that particular batch. A COA dated many months before the batch was listed for sale is worth questioning, since storage conditions and stability can change over time even if the original test result was accurate.

## Step 4: Read the actual numbers, not just a pass or fail

A proper COA reports specific figures: percentage purity, measured concentration in mg/mL or mg per vial, and results for any additional testing like heavy metals or endotoxins. A report that only states "passed" with no numbers attached gives you nothing to independently assess.

## What the identity and purity sections mean

**Identity testing** confirms the vial contains retatrutide and not a different or contaminated substance, usually via a method like mass spectrometry or HPLC. **Purity** reports what percentage of the contents is the intended compound, with impurities or degradation products making up the remainder. Most legitimate reports for peptides in this market report purity above 98%.

## Red flags in a COA itself

- No batch number, or a batch number that does not match the vial
- A low-resolution image with no visible lab name or letterhead
- Only a vague "pass" or "compliant" with no supporting figures
- A test date far removed from the batch's actual production or sale window
- Formatting that is inconsistent with the lab's other public reports, if you can find any

## Cross-checking against other batches

If a seller has been shipping the same compound for a while, look at COAs for previous batches if they are archived on the site. Consistent formatting, a consistent named lab, and plausible purity figures across multiple batches are a good sign. A single COA that looks completely different from the seller's usual reports is worth a direct question.

## Using this alongside seller research

A verified COA is one part of deciding whether to order from a seller. Pair it with checking their UK company details, delivery terms and reviews. ## What retatrutide is

Retatrutide activates the GLP-1, GIP and glucagon receptors. In Eli Lilly's Phase 2 trial, the highest dose group lost an average of 24.2% of body weight at 48 weeks. It remains investigational with no UK medicines licence, which is exactly why independently verifying a seller's COA matters so much in this market.

## Bottom line

Match the batch number, check the testing lab is named, look at the test date, and read the actual purity and concentration figures rather than a summary. A COA that holds up on all four points is a strong signal you can trust the listing behind it.
