For a 15 mg retatrutide vial, 3 mL of BAC water gives 5 mg/mL, and 1.5 mL gives 10 mg/mL. Both are valid. The right one is whichever gives you syringe numbers you can read without guessing.

This post covers the math only. Retatrutide is an investigational drug with no approved dosing instructions, and nothing here recommends a dose.

## The formula

Concentration (mg/mL) = mg in the vial / mL of BAC water added.

For a 15 mg vial:

| BAC water | Concentration | mg per unit (U-100) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 mL | 15 mg/mL | 0.15 mg |
| 1.5 mL | 10 mg/mL | 0.10 mg |
| 2 mL | 7.5 mg/mL | 0.075 mg |
| 3 mL | 5 mg/mL | 0.05 mg |
| 5 mL | 3 mg/mL | 0.03 mg |

A U-100 insulin syringe holds 100 units per mL, so 1 unit is 0.01 mL. To get mg per unit, divide the concentration by 100.

## Units to draw for common amounts

The phase 2 trial tested weekly doses of 1, 4, 8 and 12 mg. Here is how many units each takes at two concentrations.

| Dose | At 10 mg/mL | At 5 mg/mL |
|---|---|---|
| 1 mg | 10 units | 20 units |
| 2 mg | 20 units | 40 units |
| 4 mg | 40 units | 80 units |
| 8 mg | 80 units | 160 units (too big for a 1 mL syringe) |
| 12 mg | 120 units (too big) | 240 units (too big) |

This table shows why volume choice matters. At 5 mg/mL, an 8 mg dose does not fit in a standard 1 mL syringe. At 10 mg/mL it does.

## How to pick a volume

Work backward from the dose you plan to measure. Follow the steps below.

1. Decide the smallest and largest dose you expect to draw.
2. Choose a concentration where the smallest dose is at least 10 units. Below that, the tick marks are too close to read precisely.
3. Check that the largest dose fits within 100 units, or within the size of your syringe.
4. Prefer round numbers. 20 units is easier to hit than 17.

Low doses favor a weaker solution (more water). High doses favor a stronger one (less water). If you expect to start at 1 to 2 mg and rise later, 2 mL of water (7.5 mg/mL) is a middle path, though it gives less round numbers.

## A worked example

Say you add 3 mL to the 15 mg vial. That is 5 mg/mL, or 0.05 mg per unit.

For a 2 mg dose: 2 / 0.05 = 40 units.

Check it the other way: 40 units is 0.4 mL. 0.4 mL at 5 mg/mL is 2 mg. It matches.

Always run the check in reverse. It catches slips like using 0.5 instead of 0.05.

## Common slip-ups

- **Confusing mg and mL.** The vial size is in mg. The water is in mL. Nothing about the vial size changes with water.
- **Forgetting the vial size.** A 10 mg vial and a 15 mg vial need different water volumes to hit the same concentration.
- **Assuming 15 mg means 15 mg per dose.** It is the total in the vial. At 2 mg per week, it is over seven weeks of drug.
- **Using a 0.5 mL or 0.3 mL syringe without adjusting.** Those syringes are still marked in units, but their full scale is 50 or 30 units. The math per unit is unchanged, but the maximum draw is smaller.

## Also consider how long the vial lasts

More water does not make the drug last longer. It only makes each dose a bigger volume. What limits a vial is stability once mixed. No manufacturer stability data exists for reconstituted retatrutide. A commonly cited convention for multi-use bacteriostatic vials is about 28 days in the refrigerator. If your doses are small, you may hit that limit before the vial is empty.

## Handling reminders

- Use a clean alcohol swab on the stopper each time.
- Add water slowly down the glass wall. Do not shake.
- Refrigerate after mixing and label the vial with the date and volume added.
- Discard if the liquid turns cloudy or has particles.

## Key takeaways

- 15 mg vial plus 3 mL = 5 mg/mL. 15 mg vial plus 1.5 mL = 10 mg/mL.
- Units to draw = dose in mg / (concentration / 100).
- Pick a volume that keeps your smallest dose at 10 units or more and your largest under 100.
- Always check the math in reverse before drawing.
- Quality and sterility of unregulated powder cannot be verified by math. A clinician can advise on risks.
