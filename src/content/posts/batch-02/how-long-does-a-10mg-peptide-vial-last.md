A 10 mg vial lasts as many doses as 10 divided by your dose in mg. At 2 mg a week, that is 5 weeks. At 4 mg a week, it is 2.5 weeks. Water volume does not change this.

This is arithmetic, not dosing advice. Any injectable without regulatory approval, including retatrutide, has no official instructions, and a clinician should guide decisions about use.

## The basic formula

Number of doses = vial mg / dose mg.

Duration = number of doses x days between doses.

## Weekly dosing chart for a 10 mg vial

| Weekly dose | Doses per vial | Weeks it lasts |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 mg | 20 | 20 |
| 1 mg | 10 | 10 |
| 2 mg | 5 | 5 |
| 4 mg | 2.5 | 2.5 |
| 8 mg | 1.25 | 1.25 |
| 12 mg | 0.83 | under 1 |

For context, the retatrutide phase 2 trial tested weekly doses of 1, 4, 8 and 12 mg, with some arms starting at 2 mg and stepping up. A 10 mg vial would cover one full week at 8 mg, but not a 12 mg dose.

## Daily or twice-weekly schedules

Some peptides are dosed more often. The formula stays the same.

| Schedule | Dose | Doses per 10 mg vial | Days it lasts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily | 0.5 mg | 20 | 20 |
| Daily | 1 mg | 10 | 10 |
| Twice weekly | 1 mg | 10 | about 35 |
| Twice weekly | 2.5 mg | 4 | about 14 |

## The catch: the mixed vial expires

The vial's contents may not be usable for as long as the math says. Once you add bacteriostatic water and start puncturing the stopper, the clock runs.

No manufacturer publishes stability data for reconstituted retatrutide. A common convention for multi-use bacteriostatic vials is about 28 days refrigerated. That is a rule of thumb, borrowed from other injectables. Some peptides break down faster.

Compare your duration to a 28-day window:

- 2 mg weekly: 35 days. The vial outlasts the window. About 1 to 2 doses might be left over.
- 1 mg weekly: 70 days. More than half the vial would be past that window.
- 4 mg weekly: 17.5 days. Fits comfortably.

If your doses are small, a 10 mg vial may be too large. You might do better with a smaller vial or a plan for what to discard. Do not stretch a vial past its date to avoid waste. A wasted 2 mg costs less than an infection or a degraded dose.

## Dose-step example

Trials raised doses in steps. Say someone starts at 2 mg for four weeks, then moves to 4 mg. A 10 mg vial would cover:

- Weeks 1 to 4 at 2 mg: 8 mg used.
- 2 mg left, which is half of one 4 mg dose.

You cannot finish that step from one vial. A second vial is needed, and you must decide how to handle the leftover. Some people discard it, some combine it, and either choice is a judgment call to make with a clinician. Plan step-ups before the vial runs low.

## Does the water volume change the duration?

No. Water changes concentration and the volume per dose, not the total mg. A 10 mg vial holds 10 mg whether you add 1 mL or 4 mL. One thing does change: with less water, you have more room for measurement error on tiny draws. With more water, larger draws are easier to read.

## Dead space and small losses

Some drug stays in the syringe hub, needle and the vial's stopper area. In practice, you may lose a small fraction of a dose per draw. Low-dead-space syringes reduce this. Over five doses, the loss may not matter. Over twenty, it can add up.

Also, a vial labeled 10 mg may not contain exactly 10 mg if the source is unregulated. Independent testing of some products sold online has found deviations. The math above assumes the label is accurate.

## Storage affects usable life

- Unmixed powder is often stored cold and dry, but check the guidance for the specific product.
- Mixed liquid should be refrigerated at 2 to 8 °C, away from light, and never frozen.
- Write the mix date and a discard date on the vial.

## Key takeaways

- Doses per vial = vial mg / dose mg.
- A 10 mg vial lasts 5 weeks at 2 mg weekly and 2.5 weeks at 4 mg weekly.
- Water volume does not change how long the drug lasts.
- The mixed vial's shelf life, commonly cited as about 28 days, may end before the vial is empty.
- Plan for dose step-ups and leftover volume before they happen, ideally with a clinician.
