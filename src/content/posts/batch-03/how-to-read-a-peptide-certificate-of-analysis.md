A certificate of analysis (COA) is a lab report that lists test results for a specific batch of a peptide. The key fields are identity (does the molecular weight match), purity (usually by HPLC), and sometimes content, endotoxin, and sterility. A COA helps, but it does not make a product safe or legal to inject.

## What a COA should include

- Product name and batch or lot number
- Date of analysis
- Name and contact of the testing lab
- Methods used
- Results with units and acceptance limits
- Signature or lab authorization

If the batch number does not match your vial, the COA tells you nothing about it.

## The main tests

### Identity by mass spectrometry

Mass spectrometry (MS) measures the molecular weight of the peptide. The result should match the expected weight. For a peptide the size of retatrutide, which is a modified molecule with a fatty acid chain, the expected mass is specific. A matching mass supports identity but does not rule out a similar compound.

### Purity by HPLC

High-performance liquid chromatography separates the sample into components and shows the percentage that is the target peptide. Pharmaceutical peptides often exceed 98% purity. Values of 95% to 99% are commonly quoted on research COAs. Look at the chromatogram if it is provided. A single sharp peak is good, and multiple large peaks suggest impurities.

### Content or quantity

Purity is not the same as amount. A vial labeled 10 mg may contain 10 mg of powder that is 99% pure peptide, or may contain salts, water, and counter-ions that make up much of the weight. Net peptide content testing (by amino acid analysis or quantitative HPLC) shows the actual peptide amount. Many research COAs omit it.

### Endotoxin

Endotoxins are fragments of bacterial cell walls that cause fever and inflammation when injected. Injectable products are tested by a Limulus (LAL) assay and must fall below set limits. Many peptide COAs lack this test.

### Sterility

Sterility testing checks for microbial growth. Injectable pharmaceuticals must pass it. Research peptides rarely provide it.

### Residual solvents and heavy metals

Synthesis uses solvents such as acetonitrile and TFA. Tests show whether residues remain within safe limits. These are rare on research COAs.

## Red flags

1. No lab name or contact details.
2. A COA that looks generic, with no batch number.
3. Results that are round or identical across products.
4. Only purity listed, with no identity check.
5. Testing done by the seller rather than an independent lab.
6. A COA that is old or predates the batch.
7. No endotoxin or sterility data for an injectable.
8. Files that are easy to edit and unsigned.

## How to verify

- Contact the lab directly to confirm the report exists and matches.
- Check whether the lab is accredited, for example ISO 17025.
- Compare the chromatogram and mass spectrum with the numbers reported.
- Consider independent testing of your own vial, which some labs offer for a fee.

## What a COA cannot prove

- That your vial is the tested one. Batches can vary and labels can be swapped.
- That the product is sterile, unless sterility testing is included.
- That it is safe or effective in humans. Trial data applies only to the pharmaceutical drug.
- That it is legal to use.

## Pharmaceutical products do not need this

Approved drugs are made under GMP with batch release testing, and you do not need to read a COA. The need to interpret one is itself a sign that you are outside the regulated system.

## Availability

Retatrutide is investigational and unapproved. Anyone buying it online is dealing with products that have no regulatory oversight.

## Example of a COA table

| Test | Method | Result | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identity | LC-MS | Mass matches expected | Within 1 Da |
| Purity | HPLC at 220 nm | 98.9% | 95% or higher |
| Peptide content | Quantitative HPLC | 84% of powder weight | Reported |
| Endotoxin | LAL | Below limit | Below set limit per mg |
| Sterility | Compendial method | No growth | No growth |

This table is a mock example of what a complete report looks like. Many research COAs show only the first two rows.

## Reading a chromatogram

A chromatogram plots signal against time. The tallest peak is the main compound, and its area as a share of total peak area gives the purity. Small peaks before and after are impurities. Check that the main peak is labeled, that the baseline is flat, and that integration is not cutting off shoulders. Purity of 99% by area still leaves 1% of unknown material.

## Why 99% purity is not enough

Purity by HPLC measures related peptide impurities but not water, salts, or non-UV-absorbing contaminants. A product can be 99% pure by HPLC and still contain endotoxin or heavy metals. Pharmaceutical testing covers those separately.

## Bottom line

A COA is useful evidence of identity and purity, but only if it is genuine, matches the batch, and includes the tests that matter for injectables. It does not make an unapproved product safe.
