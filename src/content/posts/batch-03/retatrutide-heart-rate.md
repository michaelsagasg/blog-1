In the phase 2 obesity trial, retatrutide raised resting heart rate in a dose-related way. The increase peaked around week 24 and then declined, even as people stayed on treatment. It is a known effect of the drug class, and it is one of the specific safety points the phase 3 program tracks.

## What the trial found

Heart rate rose at all retatrutide doses, with larger increases at higher doses. At the top doses, average increases reached roughly 10 beats per minute at the peak, and heart rate then began to fall back toward baseline through the rest of the 48 weeks. Placebo showed no such rise.

Blood pressure did not follow the same path. It fell in the retatrutide groups, as expected with weight loss.

## Why heart rate rises

GLP-1 drugs increase resting heart rate by a few beats per minute on average. Reasons include activation of receptors in the heart's pacemaker tissue and changes in the nervous system's control of the heart. Glucagon also stimulates the heart in some contexts. Retatrutide combines these actions.

The later decline may reflect weight loss itself, which lowers resting heart rate over time, and adaptation to the drug.

## Does it matter?

For most people, a rise of a few to ten beats per minute is not harmful. It matters more for:

- People with heart rhythm disorders, such as atrial fibrillation
- People with heart failure or coronary disease
- People taking drugs that affect heart rate or rhythm
- People whose baseline resting heart rate is already high

Trials excluded many of these groups, so the safety data there is limited. TRIUMPH-3 and the outcomes trial will add data for people with cardiovascular disease.

## Tirzepatide and semaglutide for comparison

Semaglutide raises resting heart rate by about 2 to 4 beats per minute on average, and tirzepatide by a similar or slightly larger amount. Both drugs reduced cardiovascular events in people with heart disease or at high risk, despite the small rise in heart rate, which suggests the effect is not harmful on balance. Retatrutide's rise appears larger than these, which is why it is watched.

## How to monitor

- Check resting pulse in the morning, a few times a week, using a wearable or a cuff.
- Record it during dose increases and around week 24.
- Compare with your baseline, not with a generic normal range.
- Note symptoms such as palpitations, dizziness, or shortness of breath.

## When to contact a clinician

- Resting heart rate that stays above 100 beats per minute
- Palpitations that come with lightheadedness or fainting
- Chest pain or pressure, which needs emergency care
- New shortness of breath or swelling in the legs
- An irregular rhythm on a wearable alert

## Things that raise pulse by themselves

Dehydration, caffeine, alcohol, poor sleep, fever, anxiety, and some medicines raise heart rate. On a weight-loss drug, dehydration from vomiting or low fluid intake is a common culprit. Fixing those often brings the pulse back down.

## Athletes and wearable data

People who track heart rate variability or resting heart rate may notice shifts early. These are useful signals but not diagnoses. Rapid weight loss, calorie restriction, and stress also affect these numbers.

## Availability

Retatrutide is investigational, and no label guidance on heart rate exists. Anyone with a heart condition should talk with a cardiologist before using any GLP-1 based drug.

## Questions people ask

### Is a higher heart rate dangerous long term?

Not clearly. Semaglutide and tirzepatide raise heart rate slightly, yet semaglutide reduced heart attacks and strokes in the SELECT trial. That does not prove retatrutide's larger rise is harmless, and the outcomes trial will address it.

### Will my heart rate go back down?

In phase 2 it began falling after week 24 while people stayed on treatment, as weight dropped. It usually returns toward baseline after stopping.

### Should I avoid caffeine?

Caffeine can add to palpitations in sensitive people. If you notice a racing pulse, cutting back for a few weeks is a reasonable test.

### What about exercise?

Regular exercise lowers resting heart rate over time, and staying active during weight loss is a good habit. Check with a clinician if you have heart disease before starting hard training.

## Bottom line

Expect a modest rise in resting heart rate that peaks around six months and then eases. Monitor it, stay hydrated, and get medical advice if you have heart disease or symptoms.
