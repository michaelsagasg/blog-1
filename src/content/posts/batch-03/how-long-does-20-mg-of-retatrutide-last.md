The answer is simple division: a 20 mg total divided by your weekly dose. At 4 mg a week, 20 mg lasts 5 weeks. At 8 mg it lasts 2.5 weeks. At 12 mg it lasts about 1.7 weeks.

Retatrutide is investigational and not approved, so no official dosing exists. The doses below come from clinical trials and are used here only as examples for the math.

## The basic formula

Weeks = total milligrams divided by weekly dose in milligrams.

## Dose table for a 20 mg supply

| Weekly dose | Weeks of supply |
|---|---|
| 1 mg | 20 |
| 2 mg | 10 |
| 4 mg | 5 |
| 6 mg | 3.3 |
| 8 mg | 2.5 |
| 9 mg | 2.2 |
| 12 mg | 1.7 |

## Trial dose steps

Phase 2 raised doses in steps, and phase 3 used 9 mg and 12 mg as top doses. A person moving through escalation uses different amounts each month, so the total supply is the sum of the steps.

Example escalation over 16 weeks, using 4 weeks at each step:

| Weeks | Weekly dose | Milligrams used |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 4 | 2 mg | 8 |
| 5 to 8 | 4 mg | 16 |
| 9 to 12 | 6 mg | 24 |
| 13 to 16 | 8 mg | 32 |
| Total | | 80 |

Here 20 mg would run out in about 6 weeks, midway through the second step. This table is an illustration of arithmetic, not a dosing plan.

## The stability limit

A vial that is reconstituted has a limited shelf life, so total milligrams is not the only constraint. Peptides degrade in solution, and bacteriostatic water only slows bacterial growth. Common guidance for reconstituted peptides is to refrigerate and use within about 28 days, though the exact window depends on the product and its stability data. Retatrutide has no manufacturer stability data outside trials.

That means a 20 mg vial that would last 10 weeks at 2 mg may not be usable that long once mixed. Plan around both dose and time.

## Waste and dead space

Syringes hold a small amount of fluid in the hub. Low dead space syringes reduce this. Over many injections, waste can add up to a noticeable share of a small vial. Pulling a bit extra to prime the syringe also uses product.

## Unit math

If a vial is mixed with a known volume of water, concentration is milligrams divided by milliliters. A 20 mg vial mixed with 2 mL gives 10 mg per mL, so 1 mg is 0.1 mL, or 10 units on a 100-unit insulin syringe. Our companion posts on BAC water and vial planning cover this in detail.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Confusing milligrams with milliliters or units.
2. Ignoring the shelf life after mixing.
3. Assuming the label amount is what is inside. Unverified products are not tested to the same standard as pharmaceuticals.
4. Using a dose from a forum without any supervision.

## Availability and quality

Retatrutide is not approved. Products sold online as retatrutide have inconsistent quality, and independent testing of research-chemical peptides has found products with the wrong amount or with contaminants. That makes any vial math only as good as the label, which cannot be trusted.

## Comparing sizes and supply planning

Peptide vials are commonly sold at 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, and 40 mg. Larger vials cost less per milligram but have a stability problem: the more you mix at once, the longer it must last.

|---|---|---|---|
| 10 mg | 2.5 | 1.25 | 0.8 |
| 20 mg | 5 | 2.5 | 1.7 |
| 30 mg | 7.5 | 3.75 | 2.5 |
| 40 mg | 10 | 5 | 3.3 |

## A note on splitting

Some people split a vial across multiple mixings by only reconstituting part of it at a time. Lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder is more stable than liquid, so keeping powder unmixed until needed can protect the rest. That requires a way to divide the powder accurately, which is hard at home, so most people mix the whole vial.

## Rounding

Most syringes measure in 1-unit steps. If a dose of 0.35 mL cannot be drawn accurately, a slight rounding changes the effective dose by a few percent. For weekly doses in the milligram range, that is usually acceptable, but larger errors add up over months.

## Bottom line

Divide total mg by weekly mg to find the weeks of supply, and remember that reconstituted peptide has a shelf life shorter than the arithmetic suggests. Treat these figures as math, not medical advice.
